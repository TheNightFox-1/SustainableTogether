# Strong sustainability

Strong sustainability treats the "macro-economy as a sub-system of the finite ecosystem",
a position informed by natural science and by the dependence of basic life-support functions
on stocks of critical natural capital [R04, citing Neumayer 2013 pp. 26–28]. Weak
sustainability, by contrast, "can be interpreted as an extension to neoclassical economics",
in which those containing systems are not considered, and assumes that if investment in
man-made and human capital is large enough to compensate for the depreciation of natural
capital, sustainability follows quasi-automatically [R04, citing Neumayer 2013 p. 28].

The distinction is not a nuance. It decides whether substitution between capitals is
permitted, and therefore whether a business model can trade natural capital for financial
capital and still call itself sustainable.

R04 places definitions of business success on a continuum from profit-normative at one end to
strongly sustainable at the other, and locates the "flourishing enterprise" and "future-fit
business" conceptions near the strong end because both explicitly reference compatibility with
the relevant scientific knowledge [R04]. That is the link into
[the Flourishing Business Canvas](../entities/flourishing-business-canvas.md) and
[the Future-Fit Business Benchmark](../entities/future-fit-business-benchmark.md).

## Contested

**This is the deepest disagreement in the corpus, and it is not between fringe positions.**

- **Three pillars, no thresholds.** R10 presents sustainability to systems engineers as
  "the ability to be maintained at a certain rate or level" and as "commonly described as
  having three dimensions (or pillars): environmental, economic, and social" [R10 slide 7],
  with INCOSE Vision 2035 framing it as a system characteristic and an enterprise ethic
  [R10 slides 3–6]. On R04's continuum this is a weak-sustainability framing: no containing
  system, no critical natural capital, no substitution limit.
- **Thresholds or it is not sustainability.** R11 argues the opposite from the reporting
  side: sustainability requires contextualisation within ecological thresholds and carrying
  capacities, and that mainstream standards abandoned exactly that. It traces GRI's
  Sustainability Context principle from G2 (2002) through its displacement by materiality in
  G3 (2006) and G3.1 (2011) to no additional context guidance in G4 (2013), and characterises
  SASB's 2013 conceptual framework as a "sustainability" standard that rejects sustainability
  [R11 slides 4–24].
- **No reconciliation exists in the corpus.** R04 gives the vocabulary to name the split;
  nothing in the corpus resolves it.

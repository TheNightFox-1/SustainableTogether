## R1 · Agrivoltaics — yield, biodiversity, microclimate

### Synthesis
Agrivoltaics (APV) — co-locating PV with cropping or grazing — was formalised by Dupraz et al. (2011), who modelled land-equivalent ratios (LER) of 1.32–1.73 for half-density panels above durum wheat in southern France, demonstrating that combined land productivity can exceed monoculture references even when crop yield falls. Empirical yield responses are crop- and climate-specific: shade-tolerant C3 leafy crops, herbs, and pasture often maintain or exceed open-field yields, while C4 cereals and fruiting crops typically show 5–20% declines under ~30% shading; Weselek et al. (2019) summarise early Heggelbach trials showing modest losses for wheat, potatoes, celeriac, and clover-grass, with electricity yield raising overall LER above unity. Barron-Gafford et al. (2019) reported that in semi-arid Arizona, chiltepin pepper fruit production tripled and tomato production doubled under panels, with soil moisture retained ~15% longer and panel temperatures 9 °C cooler, evidencing reciprocal microclimate benefits. Trommsdorff et al. (2021) confirmed comparable LERs >1 across Fraunhofer ISE pilots and emphasised design dependence (panel height, density, tracking). Biodiversity and soil-carbon outcomes are far less well evidenced — most cited studies are short (1–3 seasons), single-site, and lack standardised controls — so claims of pollinator gains, soil-organic-carbon increases, or drought resilience remain largely extrapolated rather than demonstrated at scale.

### Overclaiming risks
- **Cherry-picked crops and climates.** Dryland Arizona results and shade-tolerant leafy crops dominate the literature; extrapolating to temperate cereals or fruiting crops without trial data overstates yield-neutrality.
- **Short trial horizons.** Most yield studies span 1–3 growing seasons, insufficient to capture interannual climate variability, soil-carbon dynamics, or pest/disease shifts under altered microclimate.
- **LER framing inflates the headline.** LER >1 combines electricity (kWh) with crop biomass (kg) via separate references — an accounting convention, not evidence that food output is preserved.
- **Biodiversity and soil claims are largely aspirational.** Pollinator-friendly and soil-regeneration narratives are common in industry materials but supported by very few peer-reviewed, control-matched, multi-year datasets.

## R2 · PV silver and silicon urban mining and recycling

### Synthesis
End-of-life crystalline-silicon PV recycling currently spans three industrial process families: (1) mechanical/thermal recovery dominated by glass-recycling shredding lines (Veolia France's Rousset plant operated with Reiling, and PV Cycle's network), which recovers bulk glass and aluminium frames but downgrades silicon and silver into the slag/fines fraction; (2) the FRELP (Full Recovery End-of-Life Photovoltaic) process piloted by SASIL in Italy, combining mechanical separation, thermal delamination, and acid leaching, with reported potential recoveries of ~98% glass, ~97% Si, ~95% Ag, ~95% Cu (Latunussa et al., 2016); and (3) high-value chemical/metallurgical routes such as ROSI Solar (Grenoble) targeting silver, copper, and reusable solar-grade silicon wafers. Heath et al. (2020) document that mainstream EU recyclers achieve ~95–98% mass recovery, but most of this is float-quality glass (often downcycled to insulation) and shredded aluminium; silver and silicon are largely lost. Economics remain marginal: gate fees of €300–700/t dominate revenue, and silver recovery only breaks even at silver concentrations and prices that newer, ribbon-thinned modules increasingly fail to deliver. FRELP and ROSI operate at pilot/early-commercial scale; Veolia/Reiling/PV Cycle operate industrially but as bulk recycling, not closed-loop (Weckend et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2019).

### Overclaiming risks
- **"Closed-loop" or "100% recyclable" headlines** conflate mass-balance recovery with high-value recovery — most glass is downcycled to insulation/foam glass, not new module cover glass; aluminium goes to mixed-alloy secondary streams.
- **Silver-recovery economics in 2016-era studies** assumed older modules with ~150 mg Ag/cell; modern modules carry ~30–80 mg, eroding the breakeven case as silver loading falls faster than silver price rises.
- **Pilot throughputs treated as deployed capacity.** ROSI and FRELP run at hundreds to a few thousand t/yr; actual EU EOL volumes are still dominated by bulk glass-recycling routes that recover none of the silicon as wafer-grade material.
- **LCA exclusions distort net benefit.** Recycling LCAs typically exclude transport from decommissioning sites and the embodied burden of chemical reagents (HNO₃, NaOH) used in leaching.

## R3 · EU regulation pipeline for PV

### Synthesis
The EU regulatory architecture maturing in the late 2020s will fundamentally reshape PV lifecycle design, recovery, and disclosure. The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1781) entered into force on 18 July 2024, replacing Directive 2009/125/EC and extending ecodesign to nearly all physical products via Digital Product Passports, recycled-content thresholds, durability, repairability, and substance-tracking requirements; the Commission's first ESPR Working Plan (April 2025) identifies PV modules and inverters as priority products, with delegated acts expected by 2027–2028 (European Parliament & Council, 2024). RED III (Directive (EU) 2023/2413) raises the binding 2030 renewable share to at least 42.5% (indicative 45%), introduces accelerated permitting in renewables acceleration areas, and conditions deployment on biodiversity safeguards and Natura 2000 mitigation (European Parliament & Council, 2023). The WEEE Directive (2012/19/EU) classifies PV modules under Annex I/IV category 4 ("large equipment") with an 85% recovery / 80% preparing-for-reuse-and-recycling target, transposed unevenly across Member States (European Parliament & Council, 2012). The Taxonomy Climate Delegated Act (Regulation (EU) 2021/2139) sets technical screening criteria for solar electricity generation, requiring substantial contribution plus Do-No-Significant-Harm and minimum safeguards (European Commission, 2021). CSRD (Directive (EU) 2022/2464) mandates ESRS-aligned double-materiality reporting, phased in from FY 2024 for large undertakings (European Parliament & Council, 2022).

### Overclaiming risks
- **Taxonomy alignment is a screen, not a regeneration proof.** Substantial contribution + DNSH demonstrates "not significantly harmful" against six environmental objectives — not net-positive ecological, social, or economic outcomes.
- **ESPR PV/inverter delegated acts are pending.** The Working Plan signals priority but binding requirements (DPP content, recycled-content thresholds, repairability scoring) for PV will not crystallise before 2027–2028.
- **WEEE Annex IV inclusion does not equal effective recovery.** Reported PV collection rates remain well below the 85% target in most Member States; informal export and stockpiling mask material losses.
- **CSRD/ESRS reporting can become a disclosure ritual.** Double-materiality assessments are easily reduced to compliance narratives unless paired with site-specific MRV and primary LCA data.
- **RED III acceleration areas can override biodiversity safeguards.** Presumption of "overriding public interest" risks weakening Habitats Directive scrutiny — a known tension for ground-mount PV on semi-natural land.

## R4 · Perovskite and tandem EOL and lead leaching

### Synthesis
Current single-junction perovskite modules contain roughly 0.1–0.4 g Pb m⁻² (typically a 300–500 nm MAPbI₃ or FAPbI₃ absorber), and silicon/perovskite tandems add a comparable layer above the c-Si bottom cell, so total Pb load is on the order of 0.2–0.8 g m⁻² — small in absolute terms but highly bioavailable because Pb²⁺ in halide perovskites is water-soluble (Hailegnaw et al., 2015). Hailegnaw et al. (2015) showed that simulated rainfall on damaged MAPbI₃ films released Pb at concentrations exceeding drinking-water limits within minutes, and subsequent TCLP-style testing classified crushed perovskite modules as hazardous waste under U.S. RCRA thresholds (Moody et al., 2020). Mitigation research falls into three strands: (i) lead-sequestering encapsulation using cation-exchange resins or phosphate-based getters that capture >96% of leaked Pb (Li et al., 2020); (ii) closed-loop solvent recycling that recovers PbI₂ and substrates with reported >99% purity at lab scale (Chen et al., 2021); and (iii) Sn- or Sn–Pb-based lead-free/low-lead absorbers, which remain efficiency- and stability-limited (Ke & Kanatzidis, 2019). For ESPR compliance, tandem PV would need verified leachate below hazardous thresholds after realistic mechanical, thermal and hail damage, mandatory take-back with demonstrated industrial-scale Pb recovery, and substance-of-concern disclosure in the Digital Product Passport — none of which exist at TRL 9 today.

### Overclaiming risks
- **Lab-scale encapsulation tested only on intact or moderately damaged cells.** Hail-cracked, fire-damaged, or weathered field modules remain unquantified.
- **Closed-loop recycling demonstrations operate at gram scale** with pristine lab modules; no commercial perovskite recycling line exists, so LCAs assuming high Pb recovery extrapolate beyond current TRL (~3–4).
- **Sn-based "lead-free" perovskites** still suffer rapid Sn²⁺→Sn⁴⁺ oxidation and lower efficiencies; framing them as drop-in replacements is premature.
- **"Self-healing" and encapsulation longevity claims** rarely span the 25–30-year warranty horizon expected for c-Si tandems.

## R5 · Net-positive PV case studies

### Synthesis
Documented net-positive PV cases remain rare, and most "net-positive" claims rest on operational energy alone rather than full Triple Top Line evidence. The Bullitt Center (Seattle), certified under the Living Building Challenge (LBC), produced 60% more energy than it consumed in its first year (244 MWh generated vs. 152 MWh used) via a 244 kW rooftop array, with continuous metered data published by the Bullitt Foundation and analysed by Peña (2014) and the International Living Future Institute (ILFI, 2015) — moving Economy (operational cost) and Energy capital positive, but with no third-party ecological-uplift evaluation. The Heggelbach agrivoltaic pilot (Lake Constance, Germany), monitored by Fraunhofer ISE 2017–2019, demonstrated land-use efficiency of 186% and crop yields of 80–103% of reference plots while generating 246 MWh annually, evidencing positive movement on Energy and Natural capitals (Trommsdorff et al., 2021). The Babberich agri-PV system (Netherlands) showed maintained raspberry yields and reduced heat stress under Wageningen UR evaluation (Schindele et al., 2020). IKEA's Älmhult facility claims 1.2 MWp on-site generation in corporate sustainability reporting, but operational data is not independently verified. Community-solar equity outcomes are documented in U.S. low-income shared-solar programmes by NREL (Heeter et al., 2021), evidencing Social-capital gains where subscriber bill savings are tracked — though ecological co-benefits remain unquantified.

### Overclaiming risks
- **"Net-positive energy" is routinely conflated with "net-positive ecology".** Bullitt Center and IKEA Älmhult evidence operational energy surplus only, with no documented biodiversity, soil, or watershed uplift.
- **Post-occupancy evaluation periods are short (1–3 years).** Long-term degradation, embodied-carbon payback, and EOL impacts are rarely included in "net-positive" accounting.
- **Corporate sustainability reports** are self-reported and not third-party verified against ISO 14040/14044 or LBC-equivalent standards.
- **Heggelbach yield results are site- and crop-specific** (potatoes, wheat, celeriac, clover-grass over two years); generalisation to other climates and crops is not yet evidenced.
- **Community-solar "equity" claims** often track bill savings without measuring procedural justice, ownership structure, or displacement risk.

## R6 · IEA PVPS Task 12 — lifecycle and circularity

### Synthesis
IEA PVPS Task 12 is the International Energy Agency Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme's expert task on PV sustainability, with a mandate spanning environmental life cycle assessment (LCA), environmental health and safety (EHS), end-of-life management, and circular economy across PV value chains. Its canonical output is the *Methodology Guidelines on Life Cycle Assessment of Photovoltaic Electricity*, now in its 4th edition (Frischknecht et al., 2020a), which standardises functional unit (1 kWh AC at busbar or grid connection), reference system lifetime (typically 30 years for c-Si), full balance-of-system inclusion (mounting, inverters, cabling), geographically explicit irradiation and performance ratios, ISO 14040/14044-compliant allocation, and reporting of climate change, cumulative energy demand, energy payback time, and selected ReCiPe midpoints. Complementary reports include the joint IRENA–IEA PVPS *End-of-Life Management* study projecting up to 78 million tonnes of cumulative PV waste by 2050 (Weckend et al., 2016); Komoto and Lee's (2018) end-of-life management update; Heath et al.'s (2020) review of c-Si recycling pathways in *Nature Energy*; and the *Life Cycle Inventories and Life Cycle Assessments of Photovoltaic Systems* update (Frischknecht et al., 2020b). This matters for "regenerative PV" claims because any net-positive assertion must rest on Task 12-conformant attributional LCA before consequential, biodiversity, or social capitals are layered on — otherwise the regenerative narrative floats free of defensible inventory data.

### Overclaiming risks
- **Guidelines inconsistently applied.** Task 12 guidelines are widely cited but functional unit, lifetime, and BoS scope still vary in marketing- and EPD-grade LCAs, undermining cross-study comparability.
- **Attributional dominates.** Consequential effects (grid displacement, induced demand, land-use change for ground-mount) remain contested and are typically excluded.
- **Social and biodiversity LCA still scoping-stage.** No standardised indicators yet exist for the equity and ecology pillars a regenerative claim requires.
- **Perovskite/tandem EHS data are early and uncertain.** Circularity claims for these chemistries currently outrun the inventory base.

## R7 · Pollinator-friendly solar / Biodiversitäts-PV

### Synthesis
Pollinator-friendly solar reframes ground-mounted PV from a habitat loss to a habitat creation opportunity by establishing native, flowering vegetation beneath and between module rows. In the United States, the practice has been formalised through scorecards pioneered by Fresh Energy's Center for Pollinators in Energy and codified into voluntary standards in Minnesota, Maryland, Virginia, and several other states, awarding points for native species richness, bloom-period coverage, and absence of neonicotinoids (Walston et al., 2018). Argonne National Laboratory modelling estimated that converting utility-scale solar in the U.S. Midwest to pollinator habitat could increase nearby crop pollination services on adjacent farmland (Walston et al., 2018). In Germany, the Bundesverband Neue Energiewirtschaft (BNE) released a Gute-Planung-Kriterien catalogue and a "Solarpark-Standard" with ecological minimum criteria, complemented by BfN guidance distinguishing extensive grassland management from intensive turf (Peschel et al., 2019; BfN, 2022). Peer-reviewed measurements remain thin but growing: Graham et al. (2021) and Blaydes et al. (2022) document significantly higher flower abundance and pollinator visitation inside well-managed solar parks than in adjacent arable land, while soil-carbon and ground-nesting-bird responses are largely modelled or inferred. The strongest evidence is for floral resource gain and bee/hoverfly visitation in temperate grasslands; soil-carbon, bird, and landscape-scale pollination spillover claims remain mostly assumed.

### Overclaiming risks
- **Scorecards reward seed-mix specification, not measured outcome.** Intent is conflated with impact.
- **Most peer-reviewed studies are short (1–3 seasons), small-n, and lack pre-installation or off-site counterfactual baselines.** Attribution to PV vs. set-aside grassland is weak.
- **Soil-carbon accrual under solar grasslands** is frequently asserted but rarely measured with proper sampling depth, bulk-density correction, or decadal time horizons.
- **Landscape-scale "pollination spillover to adjacent crops"** remains a modelled benefit and has not been empirically confirmed at utility-scale sites.

## R8 · Floating PV — ecological effects

### Synthesis
Floating photovoltaics (FPV) deploy modules on pontoons across reservoirs, irrigation ponds, hydropower lakes, quarry/post-mining lakes, wastewater ponds, and increasingly nearshore marine and natural lake settings; reservoirs and artificial waterbodies dominate operational capacity, while natural-lake installations remain rare and contested (Pimentel da Silva & Branco, 2018; World Bank Group et al., 2019). Empirically measured effects are still scarce: most evaporation-suppression figures (often cited as 30–70%) derive from arid-zone reservoir models or short pan-evaporation experiments, with high uncertainty when extrapolated to temperate or windy sites (Exley et al., 2021). Modelling for UK reservoirs shows surface cooling, suppressed wind-driven mixing, weakened thermal stratification, and altered ice/turnover regimes under high coverage, with non-linear consequences for dissolved oxygen and nutrient cycling (Exley et al., 2021, 2022). Reduced photosynthetically active radiation beneath arrays lowers phytoplankton primary production and can shift community composition, potentially mitigating cyanobacterial blooms but also reducing food-web energy inputs (Exley et al., 2022; Ilgen et al., 2023). Effects on fish, macroinvertebrates, and waterbirds are largely unmeasured in situ; concerns include habitat exclusion for diving birds, collision/entanglement, and microhabitat creation under floats. Robust evidence exists only for optical/thermal physics; ecological responses remain dominated by mesocosm work, single-site monitoring, and coupled hydrodynamic-biogeochemical models awaiting validation.

### Overclaiming risks
- **Evaporation-reduction figures (30–70%)** routinely extrapolated from arid-reservoir or pan-evaporation studies to temperate, windy, or biodiverse sites where physics and ecology differ markedly.
- **"No significant ecological impact" claims** frequently rest on short (<1 year) monitoring, single-site mesocosms, or pre-deployment baselines without BACI design.
- **Cyanobacteria-suppression benefits** cited without acknowledging concurrent reduction in beneficial primary production, zooplankton food supply, and oxygen generation.
- **In-situ effects on fish, diving waterbirds, amphibians, and littoral macrophytes remain essentially unmeasured.** Modelled coverage thresholds (often 30%) are precautionary heuristics, not validated ecological limits.

## R9 · Community solar and energy democracy

### Synthesis
Energy democracy reframes the energy transition as a question of who owns, governs, and benefits from generation assets, drawing on Ostrom's (1990) commons design principles to argue that polycentric, member-governed institutions can sustain collective infrastructure where markets and states fail (Becker & Naumann, 2017). Germany's Energiegenossenschaften remain the most mature empirical case: DGRV (2023) reports roughly 860 active energy cooperatives with around 220,000 members, a median project size of about 500 kWp, and dividends typically between 2–4%, with PV dominating new installations after the 2014 EEG reform sharply curtailed wind-cooperative formation (Wierling et al., 2018). In the United States, community solar has scaled past 6 GW but Low- and Moderate-Income (LMI) participation is uneven; the DOE National Community Solar Partnership and Justice40 Initiative target 20 GW serving five million households by 2025, yet NREL evaluations show subscription, credit-eligibility, and consumer-protection barriers persist for renters and low-credit households (Heeter et al., 2021). RED II (Directive (EU) 2018/2001) and the Internal Electricity Market Directive (Directive (EU) 2019/944) introduced Renewable Energy Communities and Citizen Energy Communities with rights to produce, share, and sell electricity, but transposition has been highly uneven — Italy, Belgium, and Germany advancing while several Member States lag, and "proximity" and corporate-control safeguards vary widely (Frieden et al., 2020). Equity outcomes remain weakly evidenced beyond participation counts.

### Overclaiming risks
- **Cooperative formation counts are not equity outcomes.** German members skew older, male, higher-income, and homeowner — distributional impact is shallow without targeted access design.
- **"Community solar" in U.S. markets** is often majority developer-owned with token community branding; LMI subscription rates frequently fall below programme targets despite Justice40 framing.
- **RED II/REC uptake is highly uneven across Member States.** Transposition delays and restrictive proximity/voltage rules can hollow out the directive's intent.
- **Savings-based programme evaluations** rarely measure procedural justice, governance participation, or wealth-building.

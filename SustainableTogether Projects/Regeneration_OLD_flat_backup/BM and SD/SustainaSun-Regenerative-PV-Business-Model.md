# SustainaSun — Regenerative PV Business Model Reference

**Project:** SustainableTogether (INCOSE Sustainability Working Group)
**Layer:** Business model reference architecture
**Case framing:** SolarX → SustainaSun (target state of the transformation)
**License:** MIT
**Version:** v0.1 — reference draft for review
**Currency:** EUR (base)
**Geography:** European Union (primary), structurally portable

---

## Executive Summary

This document specifies the business-model layer of an open reference architecture for a maximally sustainable utility-/community-scale PV system (1–10 MW, agrivoltaic-capable, c-Si TOPCon or HJT, designed for disassembly).

The central question the document tries to answer is:

> *Can regeneration be more profitable than extraction over a 30-year PV system lifecycle — and under what conditions?*

The short answer it argues for: **yes, but only inside a specific operating envelope.** The envelope requires (i) project scale ≥ ~5 MW per site to absorb compliance and engagement overhead, (ii) at least two bankable regenerative revenue streams operational at financial close (today: agrivoltaic land-use premium + low-carbon energy premium), (iii) a financing structure that converts long-dated material value into present-day cost-of-capital reduction (green bond / EU taxonomy-aligned debt), and (iv) a regulatory baseline of EU ETS ≥ €60/tCO₂ and CRE-style low-carbon tenders or equivalent.

Outside that envelope, the regenerative model degrades to a green-flavoured conventional model: it does not lose money, but it does not beat conventional IRR either, and most of the regenerative value remains qualitative.

What follows are the five required outputs in sequence, followed by the explicit treatment of the five design tensions and the supporting appendices.

## Output 1 — Flourishing Business Model Canvas

The canvas is presented in the FBC's seven zones (Why / Who / What / How / How Much / Boundaries / Context). Each entry is tagged with its value dimension(s) — **[Econ]**, **[Soc]**, **[Eco]** — and, where applicable, the regenerative mechanism it connects to (M1–M9, defined in Output 2).

### Zone 1 — Why

#### Living Purpose

To prove, at commercial scale, that a photovoltaic enterprise can generate competitive financial returns *while* restoring the ecological, social, and material systems it depends on — turning the 30-year PV system lifecycle into a regenerative cycle that leaves land, community, and material flows measurably better at end-of-life than at financial close.

The purpose statement is the test: any business-model decision that improves financial return but degrades ecological or social capital below baseline is out of scope.

#### Goals & Metrics

| Goal | Metric | Target (per MW installed, 30y lifecycle) | Dimension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive returns | Equity IRR | ≥ 8% (parity with conventional EU utility PV) | Econ |
| Climate restoration | Net lifecycle GHG | < 15 gCO₂eq/kWh (vs. ~40 gCO₂eq/kWh EU c-Si average per IEA-PVPS Task 12) | Eco |
| Biodiversity | Net change in species richness on site | ≥ +20% vs. pre-installation baseline | Eco |
| Soil health | Soil organic carbon under array | Stable or increasing over 10y measurement window | Eco |
| Material recovery | Mass-fraction recovered at EOL | ≥ 95% by mass, ≥ 75% by economic value | Eco |
| Community share | Local economic value retention | ≥ 25% of project lifetime revenue retained locally | Soc, Econ |
| Energy access | Subsidised supply to identified low-income users | ≥ 5% of generation at < 50% market rate | Soc |
| Supplier development | Tier-1 suppliers on documented decarbonisation pathway | 100% within 3 years of contract | Soc, Eco |

These targets are deliberately high but not aspirational: each is achievable today with existing technology and known business mechanisms, as detailed in Output 2.

### Zone 2 — Who

#### Beneficiary Actors (receive value, may not pay)
- **Local communities** hosting the installation — energy access, local employment, biodiversity, landscape stewardship **[Soc, Eco]**
- **Future generations** — material stock preserved for circular re-entry; climate stabilisation contribution **[Eco]**
- **Landowner / farmer** — improved land productivity through dual use, regenerative soil management, lease income **[Econ, Eco]**
- **Pollinator and bird species** under and around arrays — habitat creation **[Eco]**
- **Soil biota and watershed** under array footprint — reduced erosion, improved infiltration **[Eco]**

#### User Actors (interact with the offering)
- **Energy offtakers** — corporate PPA buyers, community energy cooperatives, local DSO, residential users via cooperative
- **Agricultural co-user** — farmer or grazier operating under arrays
- **Maintenance and monitoring crews**
- **Material recovery operator** at EOL (Year 30) — Veolia / Reiling / ROSI-class operator under a forward contract

#### Customer Actors (pay)
- **PPA offtaker** — primary revenue, ~70–85% of lifetime revenue
- **Agricultural lessee or co-revenue partner**
- **Carbon and (later) biodiversity credit buyer** — corporate ESG buyer for voluntary carbon, regulated buyer for compliance credits
- **Government / grant body** — community-energy subsidy, low-carbon manufacturing premium tenders (CRE-style)
- **Material recovery operator** — pays for recovered glass/Si/Ag stream at EOL

#### Ecosystem Actors (affected, often without voice)
- **Watershed** of the project site — surface water flow, infiltration patterns
- **Insect, bird, and small mammal populations** in the project envelope
- **Soil microbiome** under and adjacent to arrays
- **Supplier-region ecosystems** — silica quarries, silver mines, polysilicon manufacturing watersheds (often in Xinjiang, Sichuan, Malaysia, Germany)
- **End-of-life material streams** — currently lost to downcycling; potential to re-enter PV supply chain

### Zone 3 — What

#### Economic Value Propositions
- Stable, indexed clean electricity supply via long-term PPA (15–25 year tenor)
- Land-productivity uplift to host landowner through agrivoltaic co-use (M1)
- Low-carbon energy attribute premium for offtakers with science-based targets (M4)
- Material recovery off-take rights for recyclers at Year 30 (M3)

#### Social Value Propositions
- Local-equity participation and revenue share for host community (M5)
- Subsidised energy tariff for designated low-income users in host community (M7)
- Local skilled-job creation in O&M, monitoring, agrivoltaic operations
- Supplier development programme — capability uplift for Tier-1/2 suppliers on decarbonisation, traceability (M8)

#### Ecological Value Propositions
- Net positive biodiversity on-site versus pre-installation baseline (M2)
- Active soil stewardship — measured SOC, infiltration, ground cover (M6)
- Closed-loop material design with 95%+ mass recovery and Digital Product Passport (M3, M9)
- Documented scope-1/2/3 GHG performance below EU c-Si fleet average (M4)

### Zone 4 — How

#### Key Activities
- Project origination, permitting, community engagement, agrivoltaic co-design with farmer
- Module and BoS procurement under responsible-sourcing protocol (ISO 20400-aligned, supplier ESG audit)
- Construction with low-disturbance site preparation
- Long-term O&M, ecological monitoring, agrivoltaic coordination
- Continuous DPP data maintenance over 30 years
- EOL planning from Day 1 — recycler relationship, ring-fenced reserve fund, forward material contracts
- CSRD reporting, biodiversity reporting (TNFD-aligned), community governance secretariat

#### Key Resources
- Project pipeline and permitting capability
- Long-tenor capital — equity + green debt + community equity tranche
- Modules from documented low-carbon manufacturers (e.g., Meyer Burger, Aiko, or equivalent verified via EPD; HJT or TOPCon)
- Mounting structures designed for disassembly (single-axis tracker or fixed-tilt with bolted, demountable connections)
- Land rights with regenerative co-use clauses
- DPP data platform — SysML v2-modellable, KerML-grounded, openLCA-linked for impact accounting
- Community trust and social licence — the most expensive resource to build, the cheapest to keep

#### Key Partners
- Low-carbon module manufacturer with verified EPD and supplier-decarbonisation programme
- Recycling and material-recovery operator (forward-contracted)
- Host farmer / agricultural cooperative
- Local community energy cooperative (equity partner, ~10–25% stake)
- EPC partner with regenerative-construction experience
- LCA / DPP data partner (openLCA service provider or equivalent)
- Green finance providers — green bond underwriter, EIB / national development bank, ESG equity
- Ecological monitoring partner — university field-ecology lab or specialised consultancy
- INCOSE / open-source community for reference-architecture maintenance

### Zone 5 — How Much

#### Revenue Streams & Benefits (full Output 3 unit economics below)

| Stream | Bankable today? | Indicative share of NPV |
|---|---|---|
| PPA energy revenue | Yes | 65–80% |
| Agrivoltaic land-use premium / co-revenue (M1) | Yes (FR, IT, DE) | 4–8% |
| Low-carbon energy attribute premium (M4) | Yes (FR CRE; voluntary corporate) | 2–5% |
| Capacity / ancillary services (where applicable) | Yes | 2–5% |
| EOL material recovery revenue (M3) | Forward-contractable; NPV-small at 8% | < 1% |
| Biodiversity credit revenue (M2) | Emerging (UK only liquid; EU forming) | 0–2% |
| Carbon credit / removal revenue | Not for grid-PV under current EU rules; avoid double-counting with national NDC | 0% (excluded — see note) |
| Community / energy-access grant revenue (M7) | Yes (programme-dependent) | 1–3% |
| DPP / data licensing (M9) | Forward-looking | 0–2% by 2030 |

**Note on carbon credits:** Grid-connected PV in the EU is *not* eligible for compliance carbon credits because grid decarbonisation is already counted in national NDCs (additionality fails). Voluntary credits exist but are widely contested for renewables. The realistic carbon-related revenue is the *low-carbon attribute premium*, not carbon credits as such. This is one of the most common errors in regenerative PV business plans.

| Negative impacts to actively manage | — | — |
| Land disturbance during construction | Mitigated by low-disturbance protocol | Eco |
| Supplier-country water and waste impacts | Mitigated via supplier audit, EPD selection | Eco, Soc |

### Zone 6 — Boundaries (Rules & Norms)

- **EU regulatory** — CSRD (in force, phased), ESPR with PV-DPP (coming, expected 2027–2030 for PV), WEEE Directive (PV in scope since 2014), EU Taxonomy (alignment criteria for renewable electricity), CBAM (modules from non-EU sources), Critical Raw Materials Act, Nature Restoration Law
- **National regulatory** — agrivoltaic decrees (France 2024 framework; Germany EEG 2023 §37 special tariff; Italy DM Agrivoltaico), community-energy frameworks under RED III, biodiversity-net-gain frameworks (UK in force; EU member states in development)
- **Industry / voluntary norms** — IEC 61215/61730 (module qualification), ISO 14040/44 (LCA), ISO 20400 (sustainable procurement), TNFD (nature-related disclosures), SBTi (corporate offtaker requirements)
- **Physical / biophysical** — site irradiance, water table, biodiversity baseline, soil type, grid connection capacity, land carrying capacity for chosen agrivoltaic mode

### Zone 7 — Context (Society & Culture, Biosphere & Place)

#### Society & Culture
- EU energy security imperative (post-2022) accelerates renewables deployment but pressures supply-chain due diligence (sourcing dilemma — speed vs. responsibility)
- Rising social opposition to large ground-mount PV on agricultural land — agrivoltaics and community ownership are *de facto* social-licence requirements in much of DE/FR/IT
- Corporate offtaker demand for low-carbon, traceable energy is real and growing (RE100, SBTi pressure on Scope 2 quality)
- Skilled-labour shortage in PV O&M and ecological monitoring across the EU

#### Biosphere & Place
- European agricultural biodiversity in continued decline (EEA SOER); PV sites on degraded farmland have measurable restoration potential
- Climate change extending drought and heat exposure in southern EU — agrivoltaic shading has genuine agronomic value beyond regulatory framing
- Critical raw material concentration risk (silicon polysilicon, silver) makes circular material strategy strategically valuable to the EU, not just ecologically

## Output 3 — Financial Model Architecture

This is the specification for a 30-year project-finance model in EUR. It is parametric so that a financial modeller can implement it with the assumption table in Appendix A. The model is built bottom-up at unit level (per MWp or per MWh) then aggregated to a representative 5 MWp reference project.

### 3.1 Capital Structure

| Layer | Share of total project cost | Cost of capital (indicative, 2026) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senior green debt (EU taxonomy-aligned) | 55–65% | 4.5–5.5% nominal | Tenor 15–18 yr; DSCR ≥ 1.30 |
| Subordinated / mezzanine green | 0–10% | 6.5–8.0% | Optional, fills gap if community tranche < target |
| Community equity tranche | 10–25% | 5.0–7.0% (return expectation) | Lower than commercial equity; reduces blended WACC |
| Commercial equity | 15–30% | 9.0–11.0% | Sets equity IRR target |
| Total CAPEX | 100% | Blended WACC ≈ 5.5–6.5% | Lower than conventional (≈ 7.0–7.5%) due to community + green tranche |

The blended WACC reduction is one of the load-bearing levers of the model — it offsets ~50% of the regenerative cost premium before any premium revenue is counted.

### 3.2 Revenue Line Items

| Line item | Unit economics (base case, EUR) | Source / basis |
|---|---|---|
| PPA energy revenue | 55 €/MWh, indexed 1.5%/yr, 20-yr tenor; merchant tail Yr 21–30 at 45 €/MWh real | EU corporate PPA Q1 2026 pricing range; Pexapark Q4-2025 data |
| Agrivoltaic co-revenue / lease premium (M1) | +1,500–4,000 €/ha/yr vs. baseline lease; OR 0.5–1.0 €/MWh tariff premium (FR CRE) | French CRE6/CRE7 results; German EEG §37 agrivoltaic; Italian DM 2023 |

### 3.4 Key Financial Metrics

The model must compute, for each scenario:

| Metric | Definition | Target |
|---|---|---|
| LCOE | NPV(costs) / NPV(generation), real terms, energy only | < 50 €/MWh |
| LVOE | NPV(all monetised value) / NPV(generation) | > LCOE by ≥ 5 €/MWh |
| Project IRR (unlevered) | IRR of after-tax free cash flow | ≥ 7% |
| Equity IRR (levered) | IRR of after-tax equity cash flow | ≥ 8% (parity), ≥ 10% (premium scenarios) |

Also tracked but not optimised against: lifecycle gCO₂eq/kWh; recoverable material value share; community €/MWh retained; biodiversity unit delta.

### 3.5 Sensitivity Analysis Design

The five highest-impact variables, each stressed individually and then in two adverse-combination cases:

1. **PPA price trajectory** — base 55 €/MWh @ +1.5%/yr; stress to 45 €/MWh @ +0.5%/yr (downside) and 65 €/MWh @ +2.0%/yr (upside)
2. **Module cost** — base 0.14 €/Wp; stress to 0.10 €/Wp (spot) and 0.18 €/Wp (premium constrained supply)
3. **Cost of debt** — base 5.0%; stress to 4.0% (green-bond compression) and 6.5% (rates regime)
4. **Agrivoltaic premium** — base 1.0 €/MWh (or 2,500 €/ha/yr); stress to 0 and 3.0 €/MWh
5. **EOL recovery value at Yr 30** — base 50 k€/MW gross; stress 20–120 k€/MW

Two-factor adverse combinations to test: (PPA-down × Cost-of-debt-up) and (Module-cost-up × Agrivoltaic-zero). These are the canonical "regenerative-model breaks" scenarios and the reference architecture should be honest about them.

### 3.6 Scenario Definitions

| Scenario | Description | Revenue streams active | Reasonable target equity IRR |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Base (conservative)** | Energy PPA + one bankable regenerative stream (agrivoltaic premium) | M1, M4 (low-carbon premium), conventional PPA | 7.5–9.0% |
| **B. Regenerative (today)** | All bankable streams operational today | M1, M3 (forward-contracted), M4, M5 (capital-structure lever), M7, M8 (cost), M9 (compliance cost only) | 8.5–10.5% |
| **C. Future (2030)** | Adds emerging streams at plausible 2030 prices | All of B plus M2 (biodiversity credits), M9 (DPP licensing revenue), strengthened M4 premium | 10.0–13.0% |

The Base case is what should be presented to a sceptical investment committee. The Regenerative case is the realistic operating target. The Future case demonstrates the optionality value of building the system this way today — but should never be load-bearing in the investment decision.

## Output 4 — Viability Assessment

### 4.1 Under what conditions is the model viable at conventional IRR (≥ 8%)?

The model achieves equity IRR ≥ 8% under the **Regenerative (today)** scenario when *all* of the following hold simultaneously:

1. **Scale ≥ ~5 MW per site.** Below this, fixed compliance and community-engagement costs (€50–80k/yr) crush margin. The brief's 1–10 MW range is correct in shape, but the 1 MW lower bound is not viable without aggregation across multiple sites under a shared compliance and governance overhead.
2. **At least two bankable regenerative revenue streams active at financial close.** Realistically: M1 (agrivoltaic) + M4 (low-carbon premium). The combination contributes 3–8 €/MWh of additional revenue, which is what closes the ~5–10% CAPEX premium over 20–25 years.
3. **Capital structure includes a community equity tranche (M5) and green debt.** This reduces blended WACC by ~50–100 bps versus conventional and is mathematically equivalent to a ~3 €/MWh tariff uplift in IRR terms. Without this, the model only works in the Future scenario.
4. **PPA tenor ≥ 15 years at indexed pricing.** Merchant exposure beyond Year 15 is acceptable; merchant exposure from Year 5 is not — it forces an equity-return target the regenerative model cannot meet without crossing into the Future scenario assumptions.
5. **EU ETS price ≥ €60/tCO₂ sustained.** This is the macroeconomic floor that keeps offtaker willingness-to-pay for low-carbon attributes above the 2 €/MWh threshold the model needs.

### 4.2 Load-bearing policy and market conditions

The model fails (drops below 8% equity IRR in the Regenerative scenario) if any of the following collapse:

- **Agrivoltaic-specific regulatory framework** in the host country (France's decree, Germany's EEG §37, Italy's DM Agrivoltaico). Without these, agrivoltaic co-revenue degrades to a marginal lease bump that doesn't move IRR materially.
- **EU taxonomy alignment** for renewable electricity, which is currently the basis for green-bond pricing advantage.
- **Corporate offtaker demand for low-carbon attributes** — driven primarily by SBTi and RE100. If those pressure regimes weaken, the low-carbon premium evaporates.
- **CSRD and ESPR continuing on current trajectory** — the compliance cost is real today, but it is also the moat that gives this model premium pricing power. A weakening of the regime is mixed: lower cost, lower premium. Net effect modestly negative.

### 4.3 Minimum viable scale

**Per-site:** ~5 MWp installed capacity.
**Per-portfolio:** ~25–50 MWp installed across 5–10 sites with shared compliance, governance, and recycling-relationship overhead. This is the level at which CSRD reporting, DPP infrastructure, ecological monitoring partnerships, and community-governance secretariat costs amortise cleanly.

The model is fundamentally a portfolio strategy, not a single-asset strategy. This is a significant departure from the conventional EU utility PV model, which can work as a single-asset SPV.

### 4.4 Top three risks to viability

1. **Agrivoltaic premium regulatory rollback or saturation.** France in particular has seen significant agrivoltaic tariff competition; premiums have compressed since 2023. If the agrivoltaic premium falls to 0–0.5 €/MWh (vs. base case 1.0 €/MWh) across the EU, Scenario B equity IRR drops by ~80–120 bps. Mitigation: portfolio geographic diversification; vertical-integration into agricultural co-revenue (not just lease) where farmer is a partner.
2. **Module supply concentration and CBAM-driven cost shock.** Premium low-carbon modules from EU manufacturers are a thin market (Meyer Burger has had production stress; Aiko expanding). Reliance on this tier creates supply concentration risk. Mitigation: dual-source qualification at the EPD-verified gCO₂eq/kWh level rather than the manufacturer level — accept any module meeting the carbon intensity threshold.
3. **Community equity tranche execution risk.** The 10–25% community tranche is essential to the WACC story but operationally complex (cooperative formation, governance, member recruitment, regulatory treatment). German Bürgerenergie cooperatives have a track record; in many other EU jurisdictions the legal vehicle is less developed. Mitigation: standardised cooperative-formation playbook as an open-source artefact alongside this business model.

### 4.5 Explicit treatment of the five design tensions

**T1 — Regenerative premium vs. cost competitiveness.** Resolved by combining a ~5% revenue uplift (M1 + M4) with a ~75 bps WACC reduction (M5 + green debt). Together these cover the 8–15% CAPEX premium over a 25-year horizon and reach IRR parity in Scenario B. Below 5 MW scale, this resolution does not hold.

**T2 — 30-year material value vs. near-term investor returns.** The honest answer is that the NPV of EOL material recovery at a 6% WACC over 30 years is small (~8–12 k€/MW) and does not move the investment case on its own. The strategic value is in optionality and downside protection — DPP-enabled forward contracts with material recovery operators convert the long-dated value into a present-day cost-of-capital signal (banks and ESG-tilted equity view this favourably). The model treats EOL recovery revenue as upside, never as load-bearing.

**T3 — Community ownership vs. capital efficiency.** Quantified: community equity tranche at 5–7% return expectation versus commercial equity at 9–11% lowers blended cost of equity by ~50–100 bps on a 20% tranche. This is worth approximately 2–3 €/MWh in NPV-equivalent revenue uplift, which exceeds the engagement and governance cost (€50–100k/yr fixed) at scales above ~3–4 MW. Below that scale, community equity is a net cost. The model retains it because the social-licence and permitting-acceleration benefits, while not modelled financially, are real and have been documented to reduce permitting time by 6–18 months in DE Bürgerenergie cases.

**T4 — Ecosystem services valuation.** Carbon credits: excluded for grid-PV (additionality fails). Carbon-attribute premium: bankable. Biodiversity credits: deferred — modelled as zero in Scenarios A and B, monetised only in Scenario C. Soil carbon: not bankable; treated as a lease-improvement lever (M6) and as a qualitative ESG signal. The matrix in Output 2 makes the bankable/non-bankable line explicit so that the model never relies on non-bankable streams for viability.

**T5 — Replicability vs. context-specificity.** Addressed by making the model parametric. Every revenue and cost line item has a base/low/high range tied to identified data sources (Appendix A). A user of the reference architecture inputs their site-specific values for irradiance, lease, agrivoltaic regime, and grid-connection cost; the architecture computes the rest. The reference architecture is a *structure*, not a number.
